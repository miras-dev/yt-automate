# A day as a student in Germany

**Idea:** A day in the life of a student in Germany, told through what each hour costs: tuition is zero at most public
universities, a semester fee of a few hundred euros includes a nationwide transit ticket (37.80 euros a month), lectures start
15 minutes late (c.t.) and end with knocking, Mensa lunch under 4 euros, work up to 20 hours a week at 13.90 an hour, a WG
room for 512 euros (800 in Munich), 25-cent Pfand and Sunday closing; the visa proof of 992 euros a month shows the real
bill is living, not studying. Payoff: studying is free, sleeping isn't.

Format: long-horizontal (16:9), 6 scenes. Each scene is voiced as its own take; no sentence crosses a scene boundary.

## Scene 1 - Zero euros (7:00)

This student is waking up for a day at a German university, and the tuition bill for it is zero euros.
Most public universities in Germany charge no tuition, even for students from abroad.
The big exceptions are Baden-Württemberg and the Technical University of Munich, which charge students from outside the EU.
Everyone pays a semester fee instead, usually a few hundred euros.
And at many universities, that fee hides a bonus you use every morning. What is it?

*Scene 1: 79 words*

## Scene 2 - The ticket (8:00)

A ticket for nearly every bus and train in the country.
Many semester fees now include the Deutschland-Semesterticket. It covers buses, trams, subways and regional trains all over Germany.
This semester it costs thirty-seven euros and eighty cents a month, sixty percent of the regular Deutschlandticket.
A student in Cologne could ride regional trains all the way to Berlin for no extra cost. It just takes much longer.
The first lecture is at eight. So why is nobody running?

*Scene 2: 79 words*

## Scene 3 - The academic quarter (8:15)

Because of two little letters: c.t., Latin for cum tempore, with time.
A lecture listed as eight c.t. really starts at a quarter past eight. It's called the academic quarter.
If the timetable says s.t., sine tempore, it starts on the dot.
And when the lecture ends, nobody claps. The students knock on their desks instead.
It's an old habit, and nobody is completely sure how it started.
Now only one question is left. Where's lunch?

*Scene 3: 76 words*

## Scene 4 - Mensa and a job (12:30)

Lunch is at the Mensa, the university cafeteria run by the Studierendenwerk.
A hot main dish there often costs less than four euros. In Heidelberg it averages about two ninety.
Then it's time for a part-time job.
During the semester, students can work up to twenty hours a week, for at least the minimum wage of thirteen euros ninety an hour.
Students from outside the EU are limited to a hundred and forty full days a year.
So where does all that money go?

*Scene 4: 84 words*

## Scene 5 - Rent and Pfand (18:00)

Mostly into rent. A room in a shared flat, a WG, now averages five hundred and twelve euros a month, and about eight hundred in Munich.
On the way home, the empty bottles go back to the supermarket.
Every single-use plastic bottle and can carries a twenty-five cent deposit, called Pfand, and shops have to take them back.
The shopping has to happen today, too. On Sundays, almost every supermarket in Germany is closed by law.
So what does a whole year of this cost?

*Scene 5: 85 words*

## Scene 6 - The real bill (23:00)

For students from outside the EU, Germany puts an exact number on it.
To get a student visa, they must show eleven thousand nine hundred and four euros for one year, often in a blocked account.
That's nine hundred and ninety-two euros a month, and an average WG room would eat more than half of it.
Tuition isn't part of that number at all.
So a student's day in Germany comes down to one rule.
Studying is free. Sleeping isn't.

*Scene 6: 80 words*
