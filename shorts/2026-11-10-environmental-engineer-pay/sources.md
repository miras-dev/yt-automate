# Sources

Checked 2026-11-10. Every claim in the voiceover has a primary source and a cross-check. US figures only; the script
says "in the US".

## Scene 1 - Dirty job

- **"works with sewage, smoke and poisoned soil"** (waste treatment, air pollution control, site remediation)
  - BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, Environmental Engineers:
    https://www.bls.gov/ooh/architecture-and-engineering/environmental-engineers.htm -
    "Their work may involve concerns such as waste treatment, site remediation, and pollution control technology."
  - Cross-check: O*NET 17-2081.00 https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/17-2081.00 - tasks include designing
    remediation systems, permits and compliance inspections of industrial and municipal facilities.
- **"a typical environmental engineer earns about a hundred and seven thousand dollars a year"** (median = typical)
  - BLS OOH, Pay tab: "The median annual wage for environmental engineers was $107,110 in May 2025."
  - Cross-check: OEWS May 2025 figures for SOC 17-2081 reported at https://tradecareerpath.com/trades/environmental-engineer/salary/
    (median $107,110). The previous release (May 2024) was $104,170, so "about a hundred thousand plus" holds either way.
- **"more than double the median pay of all American workers"**
  - BLS OOH, Pay tab: "In May 2025, the median annual wage for all workers was $50,980." 2 x $50,980 = $101,960 < $107,110.
  - Cross-check: BLS OEWS national table, all occupations, median hourly $24.51 in May 2025 (x 2,080 h = $50,980):
    https://www.bls.gov/news.release/ocwage.t01.htm

## Scene 2 - The law

- **"Since the 1970s, the Clean Air Act and the Clean Water Act have limited what factories and cities can release"**
  - EPA, Summary of the Clean Air Act (1970; regulates air emissions from stationary and mobile sources):
    https://www.epa.gov/laws-regulations/summary-clean-air-act
  - EPA, Summary of the Clean Water Act (1972; regulates discharges of pollutants into US waters):
    https://www.epa.gov/laws-regulations/summary-clean-water-act
  - Cross-check: 33 U.S.C. 1251 and 42 U.S.C. 7401 (Cornell LII): https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/33/1251
- **"A factory can't just pipe its waste into a river. It needs a permit, and treatment that keeps its waste under the limits."**
  - EPA, NPDES Permit Basics: "If you discharge from a point source into the waters of the United States, you need an
    NPDES permit." https://www.epa.gov/npdes/npdes-permit-basics (permits set effluent limits the discharge must meet)
  - Cross-check: 33 U.S.C. 1342 (NPDES): https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/33/1342
- **"fines can top sixty thousand dollars a day, for every violation"**
  - 40 CFR 19.4, Table 1, row 33 U.S.C. 1319(d) (Clean Water Act civil penalties): $68,445 per day for each violation,
    for penalties assessed on or after January 8, 2025 (previous column $66,712): https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/40/19.4
  - Cross-check: Federal Register 90 FR 1375, January 8, 2025, Civil Monetary Penalty Inflation Adjustment Rule:
    https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-01-08/html/2025-00206.htm
  - Wording note: "can top sixty thousand" stays true whether or not a 2026 inflation adjustment has been published.
- **"Companies don't hire environmental engineers to be nice. They hire them because breaking the rules costs more."**
  - Reasoning that follows from the two facts above (permits are mandatory, penalties are per day per violation);
    not a separate factual claim.
- **"why is the demand growing right now?"** (answered in scene 3)
  - BLS OOH, Job Outlook: employment "projected to grow 6 percent from 2025 to 2035"; they "are expected to be needed to
    help design solutions to improve water and air quality ... amid growing concerns about pollution and the lack of
    access to clean drinking water across the country."

## Scene 3 - New rules

- **"In 2024, the EPA set the first national limits for forever chemicals in tap water"**
  - EPA, PFAS National Primary Drinking Water Regulation: "On April 10, 2024, EPA announced the final NPDWR for six PFAS."
    https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas (EPA calls it the first-ever national, legally
    enforceable drinking water standard for PFAS)
  - Cross-check: EPA fact sheet https://www.epa.gov/system/files/documents/2024-04/pfas-npdwr_fact-sheet_general_4.9.24v1.pdf
    and Morgan Lewis summary https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2024/04/epa-finalizes-new-enforceable-national-drinking-water-regulation
- **"For two of the best-known ones, the limit is four parts per trillion"**
  - EPA page above: PFOA and PFOS maximum contaminant level "4.0 ppt" each.
  - Status in 2026: on May 18, 2026 EPA proposed a rule that "upholds the NPDWR for PFOA and PFOS" (same 4.0 ppt) and lets
    systems request two more years, to 2031; it also proposed rescinding the limits for four OTHER PFAS. The script only
    names the two whose limits stand. Cross-check: https://www.ballardspahr.com/insights/alerts-and-articles/2026/06/epa-rolls-back-pfas-regulations-states-fill-the-gap
- **"That's about one drop in five Olympic swimming pools"**
  - Undark (Charles Schmidt, 2024-01-15) on the 4 ppt level: "Roughly equivalent to a single drop in five Olympic-size
    swimming pools" https://undark.org/2024/01/15/drinking-water-pfas-cost/
  - Cross-check by arithmetic: 1 ppt = 1 ng/L. An Olympic pool is about 2,500 m3 (50 x 25 x 2 m;
    https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympic-size_swimming_pool), so five pools = 12,500 m3 = 1.25 x 10^10 mL. A drop is about
    0.05 mL (common pharmacopeia convention; real drops are 0.03-0.07 mL). 0.05 / 1.25 x 10^10 = 4 x 10^-12 = 4 ppt.
    It is an illustration, so the script says "about".
- **"another rule gave water systems ten years to replace almost all their lead pipes"**
  - Federal Register, Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, 2024-10-30: water systems must replace all lead and galvanized
    requiring replacement service lines under their control no later than 10 years after the compliance date
    (2027); about 99 % of systems fall under the 10-year schedule.
    https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/10/30/2024-23549/national-primary-drinking-water-regulations-for-lead-and-copper-improvements-lcri
  - Cross-check: NRDC overview https://www.nrdc.org/bio/erik-d-olson/welcoming-epas-new-lead-tap-water-rule-overview ;
    status 2026: EPA is defending the rule in court and published implementation tools in June and August 2026
    (https://www.epa.gov/lead/epas-fy-2026-milestones-under-federal-lead-action-plan,
    https://www.nrdc.org/court-battles/american-water-works-association-et-v-epa-lead-and-copper-rule-improvements).
- **"a 2021 law put more than fifty billion dollars into fixing water systems"**
  - EPA, Fact Sheet: EPA & the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law - more than $50 billion for drinking water, wastewater and
    stormwater infrastructure, "the largest investment in water infrastructure in American history":
    https://epa.gov/infrastructure/fact-sheet-epa-bipartisan-infrastructure-law
  - Cross-check: UPI, 2021-12-02, EPA earmarks $7.4B for water projects in infrastructure bill rollout:
    https://www.upi.com/Top_News/US/2021/12/02/EPA-earmarks-74B-for-water-projects-in-infrastructure-bill-rollout/1111638480789/

## Scene 4 - The stamp

- **"only about thirty-nine thousand environmental engineers"**
  - BLS OOH: "Environmental engineers held about 39,100 jobs in 2025."
  - Cross-check: OEWS May 2025 employment for 17-2081 reported at https://tradecareerpath.com/trades/environmental-engineer/salary/
    (about 39,100); OOH 2024 edition: 39,400 jobs in 2024.
- **"They'd all fit inside one baseball stadium"**
  - Dodger Stadium seats 56,000 (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dodger_Stadium, infobox and seating section);
    39,100 < 56,000.
  - Cross-check: https://www.mlb.com/dodgers/ballpark (ballpark facts).
- **"only licensed Professional Engineers can sign and seal engineering plans"**
  - NSPE, What is a PE: state licensure laws grant "only Professional Engineers (PEs) the authority to sign and seal
    engineering plans" https://www.nspe.org/resources/licensure/what-pe
  - Cross-check: NYSSPE, What is a PE https://nysspe.org/what-is-a-pe/ ; IEEE-USA PE guide https://yp.ieee.org/pe-certification
    (PE licensure required to sign off on projects that affect public safety).
- **"a four-year degree, two tough exams and at least four years of work under a licensed engineer"**
  - NSPE, What is a PE: "To become licensed, engineers must complete a four-year college degree, work under a
    Professional Engineer for at least four years, pass two intensive competency exams ..."
  - Cross-check: NYSSPE, How to get licensed https://nysspe.org/how-to-get-licensed/ (FE exam, four years of
    experience under a PE, PE exam).

## Scene 5 - The promise

- **"the job mixes chemistry, biology and environmental law"**
  - O*NET 17-2081.00 knowledge areas include Chemistry, Biology, and Law and Government; tasks include permits, compliance
    with environmental regulations and support for litigation projects: https://www.onetonline.org/link/summary/17-2081.00
  - Cross-check: BLS OOH, How to Become One (chemistry and biology preparation) and What They Do (regulations, permits).
- **"the work is required by law, the problems keep growing, and only licensed engineers can sign off on the fix"**
  - Summary of the sources above: Clean Air/Water Acts and NPDES permits (required by law), BLS Job Outlook "growing
    concerns about pollution" plus the 2024 PFAS and lead rules (growing), NSPE sign-and-seal rule (licensed engineers).
- **"When something is required, urgent and rare, it gets expensive."** and **"paid to promise that the water from your tap won't hurt you"**
  - The video's conclusion drawn from the facts above (supply and demand); not a separate factual claim.
